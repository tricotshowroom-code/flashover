WHAT THE SCRIPT DOES, SCENE BY SCENE (observations, no quotations)
1. Information has an owner, and the owner dominates the exchange: the fence with the bonds, the man in the wheelchair pitching the bank, the lieutenant at the crime scene giving assignments, the doctor naming the price, the fixer laying out the getaway. The other person is never a second explainer. He does one of five things: accepts in a word; cuts the pitch at its first sentence; answers a why with terms or a price; refuses to discuss it; or says nothing and then decides.
2. Length is position. The man who needs something talks more (the boxman's wife, the informant in the booth, the driver who cannot drive, the kid who killed the guard). The man who decides talks less, and often in one word. When the strong man makes a speech it is because he is selling (the bank floor, the detective turning the wife, the fence selling the getaway) or because two equals are trading confessions across a table, and even then the next line is short and sideways.
3. A probe is returned in the asker's own grammar. The penologist question answered with the going-back question. The returned probe is the answer; nobody explains it.
4. A why is answered with conditions, not reasons: three clauses beginning the same way, then the terms, then "not negotiable."
5. Echo is a weapon. A man repeats one word from the other's mouth and the owner of the word has to answer the echo, not the question he asked. The investigator stops on one word of slang and the whole case turns on it.
6. The real line arrives after a deflection and a physical act. Asked what happened to him, the boxman asks about furniture; then he puts his face under the tap; then he says his wife is leaving. The reply is one word. The deflection is to the room, the act is with water, the reply is "why."
7. Familiar pairs name each other's routines. The husband says here comes the showgirl speech before she gives it; the wife says why am I even talking to you. Anticipating the familiar complaint is history on the page and needs no backstory.
8. Numbers carry the feeling the speaker will not say: cents on the dollar, response time in seconds, hours left before the man is gone, the price doubled because of the news. A man who is precise about a number is hiding what he feels in it.
9. On the phone, the eyes are elsewhere. The thief talks to the broker about merchandise while watching his friend's wife leave a motel room. The scene carries two subjects and the page says so.
10. Scenes end on an instruction, a price, a one-word acceptance or a flat fact. "Take it easy." "Be there." A time. A nod. Never a summation and never a line that tells the audience what the scene was.
11. Pressure slows and repeats, word by word, with dots between them; nerves speed up and make lists. The strong man's threat is the slowest sentence in the film. The weak man's explanation is the fastest.
12. Agreement can be the other man's sentence completed, once, between equals, and it is the most intimate thing in the film.
13. The domestic question about logistics is a question about attention, and it gets the logistics answered: breakfast, coffee, the lamb, the plate. The man answers the arrangement and misses the moment, and the woman leaves the room. (Already the Donna rule in the Guide; HEAT confirms it.)
14. An order is given whole and obeyed in action; the obeying is the reply. The crew leader says they walk; the boxman argues once; the leader says it again with "now"; no argument.
15. The second real-speech sample (sports radio): a speaker loses his own sentence, says "excuse me," and keeps going without repairing it; "I mean" and "nevertheless, man" as pivots that carry no content; a question raised and answered by the same speaker because nobody else is going to. These are the transcript standard's "sentence abandoned" and "listener naming the failure" from inside one mouth.

THE PASS (run after the v2.7 gate; report as a tally; each item either bites, is excused in the person, or passes with the evidence quoted)
H1. OWNER AND MOVE. For each exchange, name who owns the information and what the other does about it: ACCEPT (a word), CUT (at the first sentence), TERMS (a why answered with conditions or a price), REFUSE (will not discuss), DECIDE (silence, then a decision). If the other merely asks the next question so the owner can keep going, the exchange FAILS unless the person has a reason to feed him.
H2. LENGTH IS POSITION. The one who needs something talks more; the one who decides talks less. If the decider is explaining, he is selling, and the page must show whom he is selling to. If he is not selling, cut him to the terms.
H3. NO TWO CARRIERS. Two consecutive speeches that both deliver information FAIL. One becomes a reply.
H4. THE SHORT SIDEWAYS LINE. Every speech over three sentences is followed by a short reply that deflates, redirects, or gives a number, or the gate states why this listener would not.
H5. THE RETURNED PROBE. Where the people give reason, a question is answered in the asker's grammar, or with terms. Zero instances need a reason per question.
H6. THE ENDING. An instruction, a price, a one-word acceptance, or a flat fact. A summation FAILS. A line that explains the scene FAILS.
H7. THE REAL LINE. Where a scene has one, it arrives after a deflection and a physical act, and gets a reply of a word or nothing. A real line delivered cold, or answered with a speech, FAILS.
H8. THE NAMED ROUTINE. In a familiar pair, check whether one can name the other's routine. If the pair has history and neither does, say why.
H9. NUMBERS. Every number in dialogue is carrying something the speaker will not say, or it is exposition. Exposition numbers are cut or given to a document.
H10. THE ECHO. A repeated word from the other's mouth is a challenge; the owner of the word answers the echo, not his own question. An echo that is merely confirmation FAILS unless the person is confirming for a reason.
H11. SPLIT ATTENTION. On a call, or across a room, the page shows where the eyes are, and it may not be on the subject of the words.
H12. THE EXPLAINER'S POSITION. The character who explains most is the weaker one in the scene. If the strong one explains, H2 applies.
H13. SPEED. Runs of clauses match the state: pressure slows and repeats; nerves speed and list. A nervous character who speaks in measured sentences FAILS; a man in control who lists FAILS.
H14. OBEYED IN ACTION. An order's reply may be the doing of it. Do not write a verbal acknowledgment where the body would answer.
H15. ONE MOUTH. Where a speaker is thinking aloud, let him lose a sentence and go on without repairing it, or answer his own question; do not hand the repair to the listener by reflex.
